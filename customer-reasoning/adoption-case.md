# Customer Reasoning Case: Technically Successful, Operationally Ignored

## Scenario

You have been deployed to a strategic enterprise customer. After six weeks, the pilot meets its technical targets, but only 12% of intended frontline users use it weekly.

The CIO says:

> “The system is not delivering value. I think we should cancel the project.”

What do you do tomorrow morning?

## Candidate task

Deliver a 10-minute response that covers diagnosis, stakeholders, evidence, immediate action, scope decisions and executive communication.

## Strong answer direction

### 1. Stabilise the relationship

Acknowledge the gap without defending the system. Confirm what the CIO expected to change and agree on a short evidence-gathering window. Technical success does not invalidate the CIO's concern.

### 2. Segment adoption

“12% weekly active” hides important detail. Examine:

- eligible versus invited users
- activation, repeat use and task completion
- adoption by role, location, manager, shift and workflow
- time from login to useful outcome
- abandonment and error points
- whether users act on the output
- whether system use changes the target business outcome

### 3. Observe the real workflow

Speak first with a small cross-section of frontline users and their operational manager. Watch the task being performed. Compare intended process, documented process and actual workarounds.

Useful questions:

- What triggers you to begin this task?
- Which tool or person do you consult first?
- At what point would this system need to help?
- What additional work does it create?
- When do you distrust or ignore its recommendation?
- What happens if the recommendation is wrong?

### 4. Form competing hypotheses

Possible causes include poor workflow placement, missing data, irrelevant recommendations, slow response, permission friction, inadequate training, local incentives, manager resistance, loss of autonomy or no feasible action after the insight.

Rank hypotheses by evidence and test cost. Do not immediately promise features.

### 5. Select a recovery experiment

Choose one high-value cohort and one workflow. Remove the largest confirmed friction, establish user support and define a one- or two-week success threshold. Preserve an exit criterion if adoption or value does not improve.

### 6. Report candidly

Tell the CIO:

- what is known and unknown
- which evidence will be gathered
- which experiment will run
- what success and cancellation criteria apply
- when the decision review will occur

## Interviewer injects

### Engineers love it; frontline users hate it

Frontline workflow and outcome evidence should govern adoption decisions. Engineer enthusiasm is useful for diagnosing capability, but it cannot substitute for user value.

### Customer requests 15 features

Map each request to a verified adoption barrier or outcome. Prioritise the smallest change that tests the strongest hypothesis. Defer requests without evidence.

### Sales already promised one feature

Do not ignore the commitment or silently accept it. Clarify exactly what was promised, the commercial consequence, customer value, implementation cost and alternatives. Escalate with a recommendation and one accountable owner.

### Engineering says the feature delays launch six weeks

Offer options with consequences: narrower capability, manual bridge, limited cohort, delayed launch or explicit acceptance of risk. Keep engineering estimates and customer commitments visible in the same decision.

### CIO demands production next Friday

Separate production readiness from commercial urgency. Define minimum safety gates: access control, monitoring, support ownership, rollback, data-quality checks and a bounded cohort. If these cannot be met, recommend a controlled pilot rather than mislabelling it production.

## Weak-answer patterns

- “I would build a better dashboard.”
- “Users need more training” without observing the workflow.
- Presenting model accuracy as proof of value.
- Agreeing to all requested features.
- Blaming sales or the customer.
- Refusing the deadline without presenting options.
- Optimising adoption percentage without measuring useful outcomes.
